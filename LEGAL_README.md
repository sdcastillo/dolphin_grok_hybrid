## Refinements

### Obscenity

*Miller v. California* concerns sexually explicit material. Its test asks whether the work, taken as a whole, meets all three parts, including lacking serious literary, artistic, political, or scientific value. A nonsexual image of political violence ordinarily would not be obscene.

### Incitement

Under *Brandenburg v. Ohio*, advocacy is punishable as incitement only when it is directed to producing imminent lawless action and is likely to produce it. A static painting displayed in a gallery would ordinarily fall far short.

### True threats

Violent imagery is not automatically a true threat. A court would consider the work and the surrounding circumstances: does it communicate a serious threat of unlawful violence against an identifiable person, rather than political symbolism? Under *Counterman v. Colorado*, a criminal prosecution also requires proof that the speaker at least consciously disregarded a substantial risk that the communication would be understood as threatening violence. A depiction of the Statue of Liberty’s severed head does not, on its face, necessarily threaten the President. A direct message, accompanying conduct, or other context could matter.

### The gallery

A private gallery is generally not bound by the First Amendment in deciding what to exhibit. A government-owned museum also does not necessarily have to display every submitted work: curatorial choices may be treated differently from rules governing a forum opened to outside speakers. If the government has opened a forum for expression, viewpoint-based restrictions can raise First Amendment concerns.

### Other doctrines

*Hustler Magazine v. Falwell* is mainly about parody and public-figure defamation, not a general rule for political art. A caricature or symbolic image is unlikely, without more, to be a defamatory factual claim. *Texas v. Johnson* and *Virginia v. Black* offer useful analogies for protected expression and the limits concerning intimidation, but neither resolves this hypothetical.

Absent additional evidence that the image was intended and understood as a serious threat, the strongest conclusion is that it would probably receive First Amendment protection as political art.

## Governing standards

### Obscenity

Under *Miller v. California*, material is legally obscene only if, taken as a whole, it meets all three parts of the test:

1. It appeals to the prurient interest under contemporary community standards.
2. It depicts or describes sexual conduct in a patently offensive way, as specifically defined by applicable law.
3. It lacks serious literary, artistic, political, or scientific value.

Mere nudity, sexual subject matter, or offensiveness does not automatically satisfy this test.

### Incitement

Under *Brandenburg v. Ohio*, advocacy of force or unlawful conduct may be punished as incitement only when it is directed to producing or inciting imminent lawless action and is likely to do so. Provocative or disturbing expression alone does not meet that standard.

### Copyright

First Amendment protection does not establish that a creator owns copyright in an AI-generated work. Current U.S. copyright law requires human authorship for copyright protection, though human-authored contributions to a work involving AI may be protectable. The scope depends on the human creative contribution.
